Brightness and colour values are clamped between 0.0 and 1.0 when stored in a framebuffer. However, if we are in a really bright area with multiple bright light surfaces, the total sum will often exceed 1.0. All fragments will have a brightness or colour sum that gets clamped to 1.0, which results in them having the same white colour value, losing a significant amount of detail.

A solution to the problem would be to reduce the strength of the light and ensure no area of fragments ends up brighter than 1.0, but this forces you to use unrealistic lighting parameters.

A better approach would be to allow colour values to temporarily exceed 1.0 and to transform them back in the range of 0.0 and 1.0.

## HDR

Monitors that are non-HDR are limited to display colours in the range 0.0 and 1.0, but there is no such limitation in lighting equations. By allowing fragment colours to exceed 1.0, we have a much higher range of colour values available to work in known as High Dynamic Range (HDR).

With HDR, bright things can be really bright and dark things can be really dark.

HDR was originally used in photography where a photographer takes multiple pictures of the same scene with varying exposure levels, capturing a large range of colour values. Combining these forms a HDR image where a large range of details are visible based on the combined exposure levels, or a specific exposure it is viewed with.

For instance, the following image shows a lot of detail at brightly lit regions with a low exposure but these details are gone with a high exposure. However, a high exposure now reveals a great amount of detail at darker regions that weren't previously visible.

![[39-1-hdr-comparisons.png]]

This is similar to how the human eye works. When there is little light, the human eye adapts itself so the darker parts become more visible and similarly for bright areas. It is as though there is an automatic exposure slider based on the scene's brightness.

### Tone Mapping
With HDR rendering, we allow for a much larger range of colour values to render to, collecting a large range of dark and bright details of a scene, and at the end we transform all the HDR values back to the low dynamic range (LDR) of 0.0 and 1.0. This process is known as tone mapping and a large collection of tone mapping algorithms exist that aim to preserve most HDR details during the conversion process. Tone mapping algorithms often involve an exposure parameter that selectively favours dark or bright regions.

When it comes to real-time rendering, HDR allows us to exceed the LDR range and preserve more detail, but also gives us the ability to specify a light source's intensity by their real intensities.

As monitors (non-HDR) only displays in LDR, simply re-transforming the colours back with a simple average wouldn't do us much good as brighter areas would become more dominant. We can use different equations or curves to transform the HDR values back to LDR that gives us complete control over the scene's brightness.

## Floating Point Framebuffers

To implement HDR rendering, we need some way to prevent colour values getting clamped each fragment shader run. When framebuffers use a normalised fixed-point colour format as their colour buffer's internal format. OpenGL automatically clamps the values between 0.0 and 1.0 before storing them in the framebuffer.

However, when the internal format of a framebuffer is `GL_RGB16F` or higher, the framebuffer is known as a floating point framebuffer that can store floating point values outside the default range of 0.0 and 1.0. 

```
unsigned int FBO;
glGenFramebuffers(1, &FBO);
glBindFramebuffer(GL_FRAMEBUFFER, FBO);

unsigned int colorBuffer; 
glGenTextures(1, &colorBuffer);
glBindTexture(GL_TEXTURE_2D, colorBuffer);
glTexImage2D(GL_TEXTURE_2D, 0, GL_RGB16F, SCREEN_WIDTH, SCREEN_HEIGHT, 0, GL_RGBA, GL_FLOAT, NULL);
glTexParameteri(GL_TEXTURE_2D, GL_TEXTURE_MIN_FILTER, GL_LINEAR);
glTexParameteri(GL_TEXTURE_2D, GL_TEXTURE_MAG_FILTER, GL_LINEAR);
```

The default framebuffer of OpenGL takes up 8 bits per colour component. With a floating point framebuffer with 32 bits per colour component, we use 4 times more memory.

This allows us to render the scene into the framebuffer knowing colour values won't get clamped between 0.0 and 1.0.
