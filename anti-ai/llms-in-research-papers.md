[Delving into LLM-assisted writing in biomedical publications through excess vocabulary](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.adt3813)

Published 2nd July 2025

## Summary
- 15 million biomedical abstracts from 2010 to 2024 were analysed for vocabulary changes and the appearance of LLMs led to an abrupt increase in the frequency of certain style words. This excess word analysis suggests that at least 13.5% of 2024 abstracts were processed with LLMs.
- All approaches to quantify the increasing use of LLMs in scientific papers all build on the idea that LLM-written text differs from human-written text. Some train blackbox models to detect LLM-writing, others modelled word frequency distribution, and others relied on lists of marker works used by LLMs.
- Human-written texts are obtained from pre-LLM years, while LLM-written texts are generated by a set of prompts. This can introduce biases as it requires assumptions on which models scientists use for their LLM-assisted writing, and how exactly they prompt them.
- This study suggests a data-driven approach to track LLM usage by tracking LLM-induced changes in word usage and tracks the excess use of words after the release of LLMs compared to pre-LLM years.

## Results
- Some words strongly increased their occurrence frequency in 2023-2024. The study calculated the expected frequency of words in 2024 based on the linear extrapolation from 2021-2022 (skipping 2023 as LLMs were introduced). By subtracting this from the actual result, this produced an excess frequency gap.
- The study needed to stay aware of abrupt changes in word frequencies for words like 'coronavirus' and 'ebola', which were naturally changed due to real-world events.
- Hundreds of words abruptly increased in frequency after LLMs became available. In contrast to previous shifts in word popularity, the 2023-2024 excess words were not content-related, but rather style-affecting verbs and adjectives that LLMs prefer.

## Implications
- Scientists use LLMs to improve grammar, rhetoric, and overall readability. However, LLMs are infamous for making up references, providing inaccurate summaries, and making false claims that sound authoritative and convincing.
- LLMs can mimic biases and other deficiencies from their training data or even outright plagiarise, making outputs less diverse and novel than human-written text. Such homogenisation can degrade the quality of scientific writing.
