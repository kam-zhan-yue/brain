# Key Concepts

- Fracture: A planar discontinuity due to stresses. Geological fractures occur at every scale so that any large volume of rock has some or many. These discontinuities are attributed to the sudden relaxation of elastic energy stores in the rock.
	- The loss of continuity in intact rocks provides the necessary permeability for migration and accumulation of fluids
	- Geological fractures may be partially or wholly healed by the introduction of secondary minerals, creating ore deposits
- Joints: Fractures where there is no component of displacement parallel to the plane
- Veins: Fractures filled with secondary crystallisation

![[fault.png]]

- Fault: A discrete fracture between volumes of rock displaced relative to each other, in a direction parallel to the fracture plane.
	- Normal faults occur when the rocks move away from each other (extension)
	- Reverse faults occur when the rocks move towards each other (compression)
	- Strike-Slip faults occur when the rocks move side-by-side each other (shearing)
	- Dip-Slip faults refer to normal and reverse faults because fault motion (slip) is parallel to the fault's dip!
	- Oblique faults occur when tectonic plates converge at an oblique angle, causing reverse and strike-slip faulting. 
- Fault Zone: A region containing several or anastomosing (i.e. branching or reconnecting) faults
	
![[dip.png]]
- Dip refers to the direction the fault plane is inclined
	- Imagine pouring water on an inclined surface, the flow is the dip
	- The azimuth is the horizontal direction
	- The angle is the steepness of the plane.
	
![[hanging-and-foot-wall.png]]
- Hanging Wall: The wall above in a dip-slip fault
	- In reverse faults, the hanging wall moves upwards
	- In normal faults, the hanging wall moves downwards
- Footwall: The wall below in a dip-slip fault.

![[fault-terminology.png]]
- Throw: The vertical component of the fault
- Dip: The direction that the fault plane is inclined
- Allochthonous Rocks: Rocks that have been translated great distances from their original site
- Autochthonous Rocks: Rocks that have retained their original location

- Folding: Occurs when a rock is compressed and the temperature and pressure are just high enough for plastic deformation to occur.

![[folds.png]]
- Synform: Folds that concave up 
- Antiform: Folds that concave down

![[anticline-syncline.png]]
- Syncline: Any fold where the youngest rock is in the middle of the fold
- Anticline: Any fold where the oldest rock is in the middle of the fold

![[diapir.png]]
- Diapir: A relatively mobile mass that intrudes into preexisting rocks. They commonly intrude vertically through more dense rocks due to differences in buoyancy. Diapirs are hence most commonly low-density rock types such as salt, shale, and magma. By pushing upwards and piercing overlying rock layers, diapirs can form anticlines.
# Types of Faults

### Brittle Fault

![[normal-fault.png]]
In a brittle fault, the rock splits into two. The type of fault it becomes depends on the movement of the rock relative to one another.
- A normal fault occurs when the rocks move away from each other
- A reverse fault occurs when the rocks move towards each other
- A strike-slip fault occurs when the rock moves in opposite horizontal directions
- A thrust fault is a low-angle reverse fault which the hanging wall. forms thrust-sheets of allochthnous rocks over the authochthonous footwall (i.e. the footwall doesn't move)

### Ductile Fault

![[ductile-fault.png]]
In a ductile fault, the rock is not broken into parts, but is deformed, creating shear zones.
- Shear zones are regions of localised but continuous ductile displacement
- Shear zones are formed under conditions of elevated temperature and/or pressure.

### Transfer Fault
![[transfer-fault.png]]

A transfer fault is a strike-slip fault that transmits displacement between two similarly oriented fault segments (e.g. two normal faults).
- Transfer faults are usually confined to hanging walls of detached systems
- Transfer faults are lateral ramps that may accomodate differential displacement and/or strain in adjacent blocks

### Tear Fault

![[tear-fault.png]]

A tear fault is a strike-slip fault that runs across the strike of a contractional or extensional belt and accommodates differential displacement between two segments of the belt. Tear faults are usually confined to the hanging walls of detached systems.
- The yellow blocks are like rugs that are being deformed by movement underneath
- The right block. is undergoing more strain than the left block, indicating differential displacement
- The tear fault runs between the two blocks
### Listric Faults

![[listric-fault-1.png]]

Listric means concave upwards. Normal faults are generally listric faults. As the fault progresses, beds in the hanging wall rotates and dips towards the fault. Voids are filled in by broken rocks of the fault walls or provide sites at which minerals are subsequently deposited from circulating fluids.

![[listric-fault-2.png]]
![[graben.png]]
 In a listric normal fault, the main roll-over antiform can have several conjugate normal faults. This creates spaces known as horst and graben.
 - Graben is a down-dropped block bounded by conjugate normal faults dipping towards each other
 - A half-graben is a graben with a triangular profile
 - Horst is a relatively elevated block bounded by outward-dipping normal faults
 - Rifts are major grabens that extend for long distances

# Thrusts

There are two styles of movement considered in thrust: thin-skinned and thick-skinned tectonics.

### Thin-Skinned Tectonics

![[thin-skinned-tectonics-2.png]]

Thin-skinned tectonics refers to a thrust belt that occurs on the surface level and has an undeformed basement. The ramp is respected in the thrust, forming a back-limb and forelimb.

![[thin-skinned-tectonics.png]]
- There exists a sedimentary cover above the basement 
- Large horizontal displacements occur in the stratigraphic sequence, piling thrust sheet upon thrust sheet
- Thrust faults may develop in sequence either forward or backward from the first thrust
- Where the younger thrust develops in the footwall of the original thrust, the earlier, higher thrust sheets are carried forward on the later, lower ones, known as piggyback thrusting.
- If the thrusts are propagated backward, it is known as overstep
- Backthrusts are subsidiary thrusts with a displacement opposite of the main thrust

### Thick-Skinned Tectonics

![[thick-skinned-tectonics.png]]

Thick-skinned tectonics refers to a thrust plane where the staircase, flat, and ramp geometry is not respected. This is because the underlying basement is deformed. Major sole thrusts extend steeply down to the basement. This occurs in metamorphic regions

**How are plate motions measured? What do they tell us about plate deformation zones? What is a 'strain rate'?**

Plate motions are measured through a combination of geologic and geophysical techniques such as GPS, geological evidence of faults, and seismic surveys. These give us insight onto the different ages of rock bedding below the surface as well as their movement over time.

Strain rate is the rate at which a material deforms or change shape over time.

**Can you describe the differences in the expected stress distributions and patterns of strain surrounding a diapir vs a tectonic anticline in a compressional stress regime?**

A diapir occurs when low-density rock intrude into overlying high-density rock due to buoyancy forces. The primary driving force is buoyancy. Diapirs can form anticlines as they are pushed upwards into the overlying rock.

Tectonic anticlines in a compressional stress regime occur due to the crust shortening and thickening, causing layered rocks to buckle and fold. Stress is uniform and horizontal while stress is directional and planar.

**What is the difference between thick-skinned and thin-skinned fold and thrust belt?**

Thin-skinned tectonics occur when the fold and thrust belt do not deform the underlying basement. Here, the ramp on thrusts is respected.

Thick-skinned tectonics occur when the fold and thrust belt deform the underlying basement. This results in shortening around the ramp.
